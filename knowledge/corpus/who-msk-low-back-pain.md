# Triage

Most low back pain in primary care is non-specific and improves within six
weeks. The task at first contact is not imaging — it is excluding the small
number of serious causes.

## Red flags requiring urgent assessment

Saddle anaesthesia, new bladder or bowel dysfunction, or bilateral leg weakness
suggest cauda equina syndrome — refer the same day. Fever with back pain and
spinal tenderness suggests infection. New back pain in a patient over 50 with
weight loss or a history of cancer requires exclusion of malignancy. Significant
trauma, or minor trauma in a patient with osteoporosis, suggests fracture.

## Do not image routinely

Imaging in the absence of red flags does not improve outcomes and leads to
findings that alarm patients without changing treatment.

# Management

Advise the patient to stay active and continue ordinary activity as far as pain
allows. Bed rest delays recovery.

For pain relief, offer a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug at the lowest
effective dose for the shortest period, with attention to gastrointestinal and
cardiovascular risk. Paracetamol alone is of limited benefit in low back pain
but is a reasonable option where NSAIDs are contraindicated.

Do not offer opioids for non-specific low back pain.

Review at 4 to 6 weeks. Persistent pain beyond 6 weeks, or progressive
neurological signs at any point, warrants referral.
